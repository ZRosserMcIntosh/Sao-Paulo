Ballistic Dynamics · exterior ballistics

How the range works

Every round on the range is flown by Ballistic Dynamics, a C++ exterior-ballistics engine compiled to WebAssembly. The page only draws what it returns. This is the full working: the equations, where the numbers come from, how far each model has been checked, and what is still an estimate.

1Overview

A round is a state: its position and velocity in the world, its spin rate and roll angle. Every millisecond the engine advances that state with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta step through the forces on it: drag against the air it is in, gravity, the lift from its spin, and the rotating Earth. Nothing is looked up from a table of drops: a 2-mile shot is about 6,400 steps of the same equations.

dp/dt = v
dv/dt = −K(M) ρ |u| u + g + alift − 2 Ω × v,    u = v − w(p, t)
dspin/dt = ½ ρ |u| S d² Clp spin / Ix p position, v velocity, u velocity through the air (w the wind where the round is), ρ air density, M Mach number, g gravity, Ω the Earth's rotation. The step is fixed at 1 ms whatever your frame rate.

The source is open: github.com/ZRosserMcIntosh/ballistic-dynamics (MIT). The engine's own docs/physics.md and docs/validation.md carry everything below in more detail.

2The air

The air is not the ballistics engine's own: it is Aviation Dynamics' U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976, the same model that flies the aircraft on this site, compiled into the same WebAssembly module. Your temperature, altitude and humidity become a temperature offset from the standard day and a sea-level pressure whose profile passes through the station; the air then changes along the path as the round climbs and falls.

ρ = (p − e) / (Rd T) + e / (Rv T),   e = RH · es(T)   (Buck 1996)
a = √(γ Rd Tv),   Tv = p / (ρ Rd) Moist air is lighter than dry air at the same pressure and temperature (water vapour is lighter than nitrogen), so humidity lowers drag a little and raises the speed of sound. Density altitude is the standard-day altitude with the same density.

Why it matters: drag is proportional to density. A hot, high, thin day (the 2-mile scenario: 32 °C at 1,500 m, a density altitude near 2,450 m) lets the round keep its speed; a cold morning at sea level does the opposite. Compare Cold morning and Hot afternoon: the same rifle drops about a metre more at 1 km in the cold.

3Drag

Each bullet's drag comes from a published ballistic coefficient scaling a standard drag curve: G7 (a long boat-tail, the shape of modern match bullets) where the maker or Bryan Litz publishes one, G1 otherwise. Both curves are the U.S. Army Ballistic Research Laboratory's, drag coefficient against Mach number from 0 to 5.

BC = m / (i d²)     adrag = ρ u² CD,ref(M) π / (8 BC) i is the form factor: how much draggier the bullet is than the reference shape. Published BCs in lb/in² are multiplied by 703.07 for kg/m². Sierra's stepped BCs (a different value per speed band) are applied as published.

The curve's tall hump is the transonic zone: drag about triples between Mach 0.8 and 1.2. Rounds that go subsonic before the target (the result panel says where) slow less afterwards but drift more in the wind.

4Wind along the range

The true wind is laid along the range in up to three stretches (near, middle, far third), each with a speed, a direction and gusts: a smooth, seeded variation of the speed and a wander of the direction around them, blended over 20 m at each join so the round never sees a step. The flags show it live: their angle is about 4° per mph from the pole.

The drift a wind gives is not proportional to where it blows: a crosswind near the muzzle turns the round when it has the whole flight ahead of it, so the near wind counts most. That is why calling the average wind misses in Three winds.

The mirage in the scope is drawn from the same air: it boils harder in heat and still air, and leans and runs with the crosswind, the way shooters read it.

5Spin and stability

The rifling spins the round at 2π V / twist: a .308 at 2,600 fps from the M24's 1:11.25 in barrel turns 2,773 times a second. Spin keeps a long bullet pointed forward. How well is the gyroscopic stability factor Sg, from Don Miller's rule with its velocity, temperature and pressure corrections:

Sg = 30 m / (t² d³ l (1 + l²)) · (V / 2800)1/3 · (TF + 460) / 519 · 29.92 / P m grains, t twist in calibres per turn, d diameter in inches, l length in calibres, V fps, T °F, P inHg. Below 1 the round is unstable and tumbles; 1 to 1.4 is marginal; 1.4 to 2.5 is stable; above 2.5 very stable. Downrange Sg follows Sg ∝ spin² / (ρ V² Cmα): it grows as the round slows.

6Spin drift

A spinning round whose path curves under gravity does not point straight along it: like a gyroscope it settles with its nose slightly to one side, the yaw of repose (McCoy, Modern Exterior Ballistics, ch. 10). The air pushes on that yaw, and the round drifts: to the right for a right-hand twist. The engine computes it from the physics, not a formula:

αR = −(4 (Iy/Ix) Sg / spin) · (u × a) / |u|²     alift = ½ ρ |u|² S CLα αR / m The overturning moment is taken from Sg itself, so only the inertia ratio Iy/Ix (from the bullet's length) and the lift slope CLα are needed. Both are estimated for the bullet class.

Checked against Bryan Litz's formula from measured data, 1.25 (Sg + 1.2) TOF1.83 inches: the engine lands at 0.79 to 0.98 of it for the five rifle loads, without tuning (at 1,000 yd the .308 drifts 9.4 in against Litz's 11.2).

7Aerodynamic jump

A crosswind at the muzzle meets a round that is pointing along the bore: it starts out yawed into the wind, and its first swing turns the crosswind into a vertical kick. For a right-hand twist a wind from the left drops the shot and a wind from the right lifts it: the nose, yawed right of the airflow by a wind from the left, is swung by the spin clockwise (seen from behind) to point down, and the lift follows the nose (benchrest shooters know it as stringing from 10 o'clock to 4). Until October 2026 this page and the engine had the sign the wrong way round; an independent audit caught it. The engine applies Litz's published regression:

Y = 0.01 Sg − 0.0024 l + 0.032   MOA per mph of crosswind

8The wobble

Leaving the muzzle, the nose circles the path in two superposed motions, a fast nutation and a slow precession, at frequencies set by the spin and Sg, ωf,s = (Ix spin / 2 Iy)(1 ± √(1 − 1/Sg)), starting from the crosswind yaw and damping out within a fraction of a second. In bullet time the round is drawn with it, together with the yaw of repose and its real spin (the rifling's engraving turns with it).

9Coriolis and Eötvös

The range turns with the Earth while the round is in the air. In the range's frame that is an acceleration −2 Ω × v, with the Earth's rotation Ω = ω (0, cos φ, sin φ) at latitude φ:

  • Coriolis: to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern (São Paulo, 23.55° S), more toward the poles.
  • Eötvös: firing east the round is lifted, firing west it is pressed down, most at the equator. Try the Coriolis scenario and turn "Firing toward" from 90° to 270°.

With drag the sideways Coriolis drift is less than the textbook ω sin φ · X · T: drag damps the sideways velocity too, and the slow end of the flight weighs most. The engine's 3-D result matches the exact lateral equation to 0.3 % (a .50 BMG at 2,000 yd, 45° N: 10.2 in, not 11.5).

10Zero, dope and the call

Rifles are zeroed at 100 m, handguns at 25 m and the sub-machine guns at 50 m (the P90, with its reflex sight, at 100 m); the Zero menu changes it. The engine finds the bore angle that crosses the line of sight there. Your dope is the elevation and wind hold for the target, solved by flying the round through the wind you call (and, if ticked, with spin and Coriolis). The elevation is dialled; the wind is held on the reticle (the red ring shows where to put the target). The shot is then fired through the true wind, with its gusts at that instant. The difference between the call and the truth is your miss.

Scope and aim are yours. Any scope goes on any rifle:

  • a mil-dot scope (3–32×, 0.1 mil clicks);
  • an MOA scope (¼ MOA clicks);
  • the ACOG 4×32 (TA31 class, 7° field);
  • the PSO-1;
  • the PU.
By default each rifle wears its own: the Mosin the PU, the SVD the PSO-1, the 5.56 rifle the ACOG, the rest a mil scope. The dope card speaks the scope's language: mils or MOA to dial, a drum mark and a hold, or an ACOG mark and a hold.

In Assisted aim, the dope for your wind call is set and held for you: dialled, or drummed, or held on the marks. The view shows the sight picture: the crosshair where the shooter puts it, and the red ring on the target's centre.

In Manual aim you do it:

  • turn the elevation turret (or the drum) and the windage turret, click by click;
  • put the crosshair where you think it should go, by dragging in the scope or with the arrow keys.
Dial the dope sets the elevation from your card and leaves the wind to you. A dashed ring can show where the call says to aim. The shot leaves at the zero plus the turrets plus where the crosshair is.

The ACOG is not dialled for range. Its illuminated chevron's tip is the 100 m zero, and the bars below it are for 400 to 800 m. Each bar is as wide as a man's shoulders (19 in) at its range, so it also tells you the range. The engine places the bars from this load's trajectory in standard air; the real reticle is engraved for M855 from a 20 in barrel. The 7° field is published. The chevron's size and the ¼ MOA clicks are estimated.

The Soviet scopes. The Mosin M91/30 wears the 3.5× PU (1940) and the SVD the 4× PSO-1 (1964). Neither dials in mils. The elevation drum is engraved in hundreds of metres (PU 1 to 13, PSO-1 1 to 10), and holds are read in thousandths, 1/6000 of a circle (1.047 mrad). The engine engraves each drum for its own load in the firing tables' normal conditions: 15 °C, 750 mmHg and still air. Your dope is the nearest drum mark, plus what the day's air and wind leave over, held on the reticle. The PU has no scales: it shows a pointed post, whose tip is the aim point, between two heavy bars, and everything beyond the drum is held by eye. The PSO-1 has the aiming chevron; three chevrons below it for 1,100, 1,200 and 1,300 m with the drum on 10, which the engine places from the 7N1's own trajectory; a wind and lead scale with a mark every thousandth out to 10 each side; and, bottom left, a rangefinder that fits a 1.7 m man between its base line and the curve at 200 to 1,000 m. The PSO-1's 6° field is published. The PU's 4.5° field and both reticles' line widths are estimated from photographs.

Pistols and sub-machine guns

The short range runs on the same engine as the 2-mile shot: the same drag tables, air, spin and Earth. The guns are a Glock 17, a 1911 Government, a Smith & Wesson 686, the FN Five-seveN, the MP5A3, the P90 and the Thompson M1A1, each with its barrel, twist and sight height, and each firing its own load at the speed its barrel gives (the MP5's 9 mm at 400 m/s, the Glock's at 350; the P90's 5.7 mm faster than the Five-seveN's). What shows up close:

  • Hitting low up close. The sights sit 2 to 4 cm over the bore, and the bore is tipped up to meet the line of sight at the zero. Inside the zero the round is still climbing to it, so at 7 m a Glock zeroed at 25 m hits about 7 mm low; an MP5 zeroed at 50 m, its sights 3.6 cm over the bore, hits about 1 cm low at 10 m.
  • The pistol rainbow. A 9 mm round leaves at about Mach 1 and loses a quarter of its speed by 200 m: zeroed at 25 m it is 27 cm low at 100 m and over 1.6 m low at 200 m.
  • Twist both ways. The 1911 is rifled left-hand, so its .45 drifts left and a crosswind jumps it the other way; the Thompson fires the same bullet with a right-hand twist. At pistol ranges these are millimetres (estimated spin terms; Miller's rule and Litz's jump formula are stretched by stubby pistol bullets, whose stability factor of 8 to 13 is far past the rifles they were fitted to).
  • Full auto. Hold Space with the MP5 (about 800 rounds a minute), the P90 (900) or the Thompson (700): each round is its own flight, leaving at the gun's cyclic rate. A semi-automatic fires once per press.

Sights. Iron sights and the red dot have no magnification: you see the target as the eye does, with a post in a notch (a centre hold, the post's top on the aim point; sizes estimated from service pistols) or a 2 MOA dot. Everything is held: the dope card gives the hold in MOA and in centimetres at the target.

The turrets

In manual aim the turrets click as real ones do: 0.1 mil or ¼ MOA a click (the Soviet drums by hundreds of metres), with about 30 mil (100 MOA) of elevation and 10 mil (35 MOA) of windage each way, and 10 mil or 15 MOA a turn (typical long-range scopes; estimated). Type a value and it goes to the nearest click; tap or hold the arrows to turn them (holding spins up to a hundred clicks a second; Shift turns ten). Under each turret: the clicks dialled, the turn you are on, and how many clicks you are from your dope card. A card past the scope's travel (the 2-mile .50 needs about 45 mil) says so: dial what the turret has and hold the rest on the reticle. The card's elevations are shown to the click you would dial, so the turret and the card read the same numbers. Iron sights, the red dot and the ACOG have nothing to dial for range, so their turrets are not shown: you hold, and the dashed ring shows where.

10bThe duel

Challenge a friend makes a link that carries this range: the distance, the air, the latitude, the direction, and a wind rolled at random and hidden from both of you. Your friend opens it and sits at the far end, facing you down the same range; their browser answers with a reply link they send back, and when you open it (or paste it in), the two browsers connect directly to each other (WebRTC, with public STUN servers to find each other; no game server, and no relay, so two players both behind strict carrier networks may not connect).

Over Railgun Meet it is one link and you can talk. The range first asks Railgun Meet (meet.railgun.chat) for a hostless voice channel for the two of you; when Meet answers, the link is all your friend needs, LiveKit's servers carry the shots (on their own data topic, rg.duel.v1) and your voices (Mic, or V; off until you turn it on), and it connects through any network. When Meet does not answer, the duel falls back to the direct link above by itself.

Only the trigger pull travels. The engine flies a round bit for bit the same on every machine, so a shot is sent as what left the muzzle: the cartridge, the bore's elevation and azimuth, and the instant. Both browsers fly that round from the shooter's end through the same air and the same gusts, and both find the same hit; the shooter's browser also sends what it found, and the other checks it. The clocks are matched by pinging (the shortest round trip wins), and the duel runs on the challenger's clock at real speed. You see your friend's muzzle flash at once and hear the report after the sound has crossed the range; a supersonic round passing within a few metres cracks. What can be hit is the head over the sights (a 22 cm sphere, 2 points) and the shoulders over the sandbags (44 cm wide, 1 point); first to 3. Firing east and west, the two of you get different Coriolis and Eötvös: the duel is fair only on a north–south range at the equator, as in life. After a hit both sides hold fire for 4 s; K replays the last hit in bullet time.

Hidden duels and duels by message

Hide us. Everything about a duel's world comes from its id alone, worked out the same in both browsers and not written in the link: the wind, and in a hidden duel the true distance (65 to 100 % of the one set), how far to one side each shooter is, and nine empty sandbag emplacements around each. The range's distance boards are taken down, the flags run on past where anyone could be, and nothing on the page points at the other shooter: no target ring, no dope for "the" distance, no report of how far a miss was from them. You search at low power, find the emplacement with a head over it, and range it on the reticle: a head is 22 cm and the shoulders 44 cm, and size in millimetres over mils is metres. Each browser tells the other where its scope points five times a second; a scope within 12 mil of you glints (a stand-in for sunlight off the objective: the page has no sun angle yet).

By message. A shot is six numbers (the cartridge, the bore's elevation and azimuth, the instant, the round's seed and stream), and both browsers fly it the same, so a duel can travel as a link with no connection at all. The link carries every shot so far, compressed; opening it flies them all again to work out the score, shows the last one coming in, and gives you your turn. A link cannot claim a hit its shot did not make (it can still be edited to aim better than its sender did: there is no referee).

The shooter

The crosshair is not still. Its wander is three slow sines an axis, scaled to the position's wobble (1 SD: bench 0.06 MOA, prone on a bipod 0.3, prone with a sling 0.7, sitting 1.8, kneeling 3, standing 6.5; estimated from marksmanship manuals' wobble areas), with the breath's rise and fall every four seconds and the pulse's kick on top. Holding the breath settles the rifle at the natural pause and steadies the wobble to 55 % for about six seconds; held longer it grows, and at twelve you must breathe. The shot leaves along wherever the crosshair is at that instant, so that angle is part of a shared link and of a duel's shot. The recoil's jump in the scope is the look only, a third of a mil for each kg·m/s of the bullet's momentum at the bench (estimated).

The bullets

Each bullet is turned from its own profile: a tangent ogive from the shoulder to the meplat, the bearing surface, an 8.5° boat tail or a flat base, with the library's diameter and overall length; nose and boat-tail lengths are estimated from the makers' drawings. The rifling's marks are in the surface (the right number of lands, at the helix angle of the barrel's twist), not standing out of it. Tips: open-tip match, polymer (Hornady's red, FN's blue), the M855's green paint, a full metal jacket's dome, a hollow point's cavity, a soft point's lead.

11Bullet time

Slow motion never changes the physics. The engine flies the whole shot first, in its fixed 1 ms steps; the page then shows it at any speed. Between steps the round's state comes from a cubic Hermite interpolation of the step states (positions and velocities at both ends), within 20 µm of a run with steps twenty times finer, so a camera at 1/1,000 speed sees the round glide, never jump. In Bullet time the speed follows the round: slow out of the muzzle, up towards real time down the range (never faster: a 6-second flight takes at least 7 s), then down to about 1/2,500 for the last metres.

Many rounds at once. Fire again while a round is still in the air, and the next one goes too. The engine keeps every round as its own flight, in the air and wind it was fired into, and each flies exactly as it would alone: the engine's tests check this to the last bit. One clock runs the whole range. Bullet time follows the latest round, and the cameras ride with it. A rifle can only fire so fast. A bolt has to be worked (about 1.2 to 1.6 s), while a semi-automatic is ready again in 0.12 to 0.25 s; both figures are estimated. A shot asked for sooner waits its turn, so in bullet time you can press the trigger quickly and the rounds still leave the muzzle at the rifle's real cadence, one after another.

12The trace, the shock and the mirage

A spotter behind the rifle watches the round go downrange: not the bullet, too small and too fast, but its trace, the turbulent wake it leaves, a thin tube of stirred, slower and warmer air. Warm air has a lower refractive index, so the tube bends the light behind it like a weak lens. The engine sheds that wake along the path every fraction of a metre, where the round was and with its speed and drag then, ages it as the round flies on, and lets the wind carry it. Each stretch spreads as the self-similar far wake of an axisymmetric body (Pope, Turbulent Flows):

θ = √(CDA / 2π)    r½ = a θ (x/θ)1/3    u₀/U = b (x/θ)−2/3,   x = U · age The wake widens as the cube root of its age and fades as its two-thirds power: a .308's trace is a few centimetres wide after 50 m. a and b are estimated at the order of measured values.

What bends the light is the heat. The drag's work stays in the wake as heat, so its centreline is warmer by ΔT/T = (u₀/U)(γ − 1)M², and the index there falls by 2.9×10⁻⁴ · ΔT/T · ρ/1.225. A fast round leaves a far hotter wake: for its momentum, a .308 at Mach 2.3 heats its wake 8 times as much as a .45 at Mach 0.8. Above Mach 1 part of the drag is wave drag, which leaves as the shocks' sound (the crack), not as heat in the wake; the wake is given only the drag the shape has at Mach 0.8 (estimated), about 40 % of a boat-tail's drag at Mach 2.3.

turn ≈ 2.9×10⁻⁴ · index · l / r A ray that runs a length l through a stretch of width r turns by about this angle. Seen from the side, l is the wake's width and the turn is nothing you could see. Seen end-on from behind the rifle, the ray runs along the wake and the stretches add up: a .308's trace turns the light by a few tenths of a milliradian in its first few hundred metres, many times the mirage, and fades as the round slows and its wake cools. The page draws exactly that turn, in pixels of the view you are looking through. The trace is seen against textured ground or mirage; against plain sky it shows nothing, as in life.

No white smoke. The trace is refraction only. Bullets do leave a visible trail in near-saturated air, where the cooling behind the round condenses water; the page fogs the trace only above 85 % humidity.

The shock cone (the bullet camera only). The Mach cone's half-angle is asin(1/M), its axis along the round's velocity through the air (not its nose, which wobbles), its apex at the nose. Close to Mach 1 the shock stands off the nose as a bow wave; the drawn cone's half-angle is held at 75° there. Its strength is Whitham's far-field rule for a projectile, Δp/p = 0.53 (M² − 1)1/8 d / (r3/4 l1/4), a few per cent a hand's width from a rifle bullet, falling as r−3/4. To the naked eye that turns light by only a few tenths of a milliradian at the cone's edge, invisible from the bullet camera; it is drawn 20 times stronger, as a schlieren photograph shows it. That gain is presentation, not physics.

The mirage (the scope). Heat rising from sunlit ground makes the image dance by an angle, not by a share of the screen: about 30 microradians RMS over a kilometre of sunny ground (the angle-of-arrival spread 2.91 Cn² L D−1/3, for Cn² near 10⁻¹³ m−2/3 and a 5 cm objective), growing as the square root of the distance. So it shows more at 25× than at 4×, and its cells, fixed in size in the sky, fill more of a narrower view. It boils in still air, runs and leans with a light crosswind, and washes out above about 4 m/s. The air temperature stands in for the sun on the ground (estimated): the page has no time of day or cloud yet.

Tracers are optional, in red (NATO M62 and M856), green (seen on Russian 7.62×54R), orange, yellow or white. The trace lights about 30 m out (the M62 datasheet gives 13 to 140 m). By default it stays lit all the way to the impact, so you can follow any shot to the target. Untick that option to make it burn out by range for each calibre, as real tracers do. The 7.62 NATO figure of at least 775 m is published (RWS M62 datasheet); the others are estimated: 5.56 mm 800 m, 6.5 mm 900 m, 7.62×54R and .338 1,000 m, .408 1,200 m, .50 1,600 m and 9 mm 250 m. Behind the glowing base the page draws the streak the eye keeps, the last 0.07 s of flight. The round flies as the ball load does: tracers are built to match ball out to burnout, and the mass the composition burns off is not modelled.

Hit markers are optional: an X flash on a hit (red when the round reached the dummy), and on the reticle a mark where each of your last six shots went, so you can walk your next shot in. Set them to every impact to see the misses too.

13The dummy and the glass

Choose Crash dummy behind glass as the target (or the Through a windscreen scenario) and a hit opens the impact in the Liquids Lab, where three engines take over from each other:

  1. Ballistic Dynamics (this range) flies the shot and hands over the round as it arrives: its speed, its mass and diameter, and the angle it comes in at (down by the descent angle, sideways by the drift).
  2. Glass Dynamics (the Glass Lab) breaks the pane: the round crushes its way through each ply, tears the interlayer of a laminate, and loses speed doing it.
  3. Liquid Dynamics takes the round through the crash dummy's simulant at what is left of that speed: the entry and exit wounds, the back spatter onto the broken glass and the forward spatter, in bullet time.

The armoured V8. The Rosser V8 from the driving sim, with a crash dummy at the wheel behind its armoured windscreen (32 mm of glass-clad polycarbonate, raked 69.5°, the Glass Lab's layup). The range follows the round from the target plane on to the raked pane and fires it into Glass Dynamics here, at its real angle and with its own mass and radius: the pane stops it, it glances off, or it gets through and the Liquids Lab takes it into the driver. At that rake a round from the front strikes at about 21° to the glass and sees nearly three times the thickness.

A .308 from 600 m, for example, arrives at 497 m/s, leaves the windscreen at 473 m/s and the dummy at 412 m/s. What does not cross over yet: the glass is broken by the Glass Lab's nearest bullet of the same class (its 9 mm, 7.62 mm or .50 ball, at the arriving speed), and the round's yaw and spin at arrival are not handed over. Moving the projectile side of the glass impact into Ballistic Dynamics is that engine's Milestone 8.

14Same shot, every machine

The engine's maths uses only correctly rounded operations: its own exp, log, sin, cos and atan (fdlibm's algorithms), no fused multiply-adds. The same inputs give the same bits on Linux, macOS, Windows and in every browser; continuous integration checks three golden hashes on all of them. That is why a Share link fires exactly the same shot for whoever opens it: the same gust at the same instant, the same impact to the micrometre.

16What is estimated

estimated The lift slope, the inertia ratio and the roll damping behind spin drift are typical values for the bullet class, not measured for each bullet; the bullet lengths too. The spin drift lands within about 20 % of Litz.
not yet Every round of a load is the same round: muzzle-velocity spread, powder temperature (the temperature here changes the air, not the powder), ballistic-coefficient spread and group size are the engine's next milestone. Transonic dynamic instability (a stable round starting to wobble as it goes subsonic) is not modelled; gyroscopic instability (too slow a twist) is. The range is flat and the Earth's curvature is left out: at 3.2 km the target sits 0.8 m below the shooter's horizon, but the line of sight follows it down and gravity's tilt along the way averages out, so it moves the impact by well under a millimetre. A single G1 BC carries the .50 BMG to 2 miles: past about 2 km, where it slows through Mach 1, a boat-tail's real drag rises faster than a constant G1 BC says, so the 2-mile drop is likely a few mil short (with the only G7 figure found, 0.510 from a retailer, it is 48.5 mil, not 45.3). A measured curve for the bullet is an open item.
estimated The 7.62×54R loads use secondary-source BCs: Type L light ball G1 0.338, 7N1 G1 0.411 and G7 0.206, marked "citation needed" where they were found. The Mosin's 865 m/s is the figure usually quoted. The SVD's 830 m/s may belong to the later 7N14. Both rifles' sight heights are estimated, and the PSO-1's offset to the left of the bore is not modelled. Checking against the Soviet firing tables is an open item.
estimated The handguns' and sub-machine guns' sight heights; the twists of the Five-seveN, the 686 and the Thompson M1A1 (and the Glock's and the 1911's come from secondary sources); the P90's SS197SR speed (between FN's figures for the 4.8 in Five-seveN and the 16 in PS90); the 5.7 mm V-MAX's BC (Hornady's for its 40 gr .224 V-MAX: FN publishes none); the MP5's 400 m/s assumes 124 gr ball (H&K does not name the load). The iron sights' sizes are typical, not any one gun's.
published Every number in the engine's data carries its source; manufacturers' figures came through the pages named in the validation table where the makers' own sites could not be reached from the build.
