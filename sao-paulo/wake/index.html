Wake Turbulence Lab

A 172 is on a 3° final for runway 35L at Congonhas, 5 km out. A 737 cleared for the parallel runway lines up on the wrong one, overtakes it 80 kt faster and passes just above it, then goes around. Nothing here is animated: both aircraft are flown by Aviation Dynamics, the flight engine behind Fly the 172, compiled to WebAssembly, and the 172 flies through the jet's wake as the engine computes it.

Controls

  • Pick a scenario or set one up, then Fly it (Space). R flies it again.
  • Slow-mo (A) slows the clock by itself while the 172 is in the wake, and back up after; the buttons beside it set the speed by hand, down to 1/50. P pauses.
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom, C cycles the camera: chase, the 172's cockpit, the tower, beside the two, and behind the jet.
  • Smoke (M) puts smoke on the jet's wingtips, carried by the air the engine computes, so you see the wake roll up, sink and spread. Vortices (V) draws the wake's vortex lines themselves, brighter where they are stronger.

What is solved

  • The wake: each aircraft sheds an unsteady vortex lattice from its lifting surfaces, as it actually loads them, every step. The sheets roll up into a pair of vortices about π/4 of the span apart that sink at Γ/(2π b₀), about 1.7 m/s behind a 737 on approach; they grow viscous cores, the ground's mirror images spread them apart near the ground, and turbulence decays them (Sarpkaya's P2P model). A 737 on final sheds about 300 m²/s.
  • The encounter: the 172's wings are lifting strips that each feel the air where they are, so a vortex beside the wing lifts one wing and pushes the other down: a rolling moment that, close in, is more than full aileron can hold. Directly under the pair it is the downwash between them instead, and the 172 sinks rather than rolls. That is why the side offset matters so much.
  • The pilots: both are the same autopilot-like pilot (a line follower on the truth state): it holds the wings level, the line and the speed with the throttle, and the jet's pilot goes around with take-off thrust and a 5° climb. A human pilot reacts later; the roll here is a best case.
  • Separation: controllers keep a LIGHT aircraft 5 NM behind a MEDIUM such as the 737 (6 NM behind a HEAVY) on approach for exactly this. Here it is a few dozen metres.

The phone number

When a controller sees a pilot deviation, the tower reads the crew a scripted notice, the "Brasher warning": possible pilot deviation, advise you call a number. It is the start of an investigation. The number shown here is fictional.

Honest limits

The wake model is a vortex lattice with an engineering core and decay model, not a CFD solution; the vortex strength and sink rate are what theory and flight measurements give, but the exact roll in a given encounter depends on where the cores pass to the metre, as it does in life. The ground is flat at the field's elevation, and there is no city to hit.
